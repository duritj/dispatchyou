Dispatcher or broker? Who does what in freight

A load does not appear on a truck by magic. Someone finds it, someone agrees the rate, and someone tells the driver where to be. Those jobs get mixed together in everyday talk. They are not the same job.

What a dispatcher does for a carrier

A dispatcher works on the carrier's side. On this site, that means looking for freight, talking through a rate, planning the day, and keeping the driver posted. The carrier is still the one hauling. The dispatcher is the desk that handles the phone calls and the paperwork the driver cannot do while rolling.

DispatchYou describes its desk that way: load search, route planning, check-ins, permits and paperwork, and rate talks for spot and contract freight. The dispatcher is not a second trucking company.

What a broker does

A freight broker sits between a shipper and a carrier. The broker arranges transportation and, in the usual setup, holds broker authority to do that. The broker's customer is often the shipper. The carrier's customer, in a brokered load, is often the broker.

That is why the rate confirmation, the broker's credit, and who pays the bill matter. DispatchYou's FAQ says the desk checks that a broker or shipper has an A, B, or C rating with the carrier's factoring company before booking. The desk is still working for the carrier when it does that.

Why the difference matters

If you hire a dispatch desk, you are asking them to represent your authority. If you book with a broker, you are hauling freight that broker controls. Mixing the two up leads to bad assumptions about who can invoice, who can solicit a shipper, and who is allowed to hold yourself out as arranging transportation for the public.

This is general industry background, not legal advice. Authority rules change, and your own compliance contact is the right person for a ruling on your operation.

Semi-truck safety habits every driver should keep

Most bad days on the road start small: a light that was out at the yard, a following distance that shrank in the rain, a log that was "close enough." Habits are how those stay small.

Before the wheels turn

Walk the truck the same way every time. Lights, tires, leaks, coupling, and the load's securement are the parts that fail in public. A written pre-trip is not paperwork for its own sake. It is the list that keeps you from skipping the side you cannot see from the driver's seat.

On the road

  • Leave more room than feels necessary, then add more in the rain or behind a car-hauler.
  • Assume the car in the right lane does not know you need the ramp's full length.
  • Stop when you are tired. Federal hours-of-service rules exist because fatigue does not feel like fatigue until late.

DispatchYou lists safety assistance as one of the support services around dispatch. That is office-side help. It does not replace the walk-around or the decision to shut down.

Rules and best practice change. Check current FMCSA guidance and your carrier's own policy before you treat any habit list as the whole standard.

Driving a truck after dark

Night freight pays some of the bills because the highways are quieter. Quieter is not the same as easier. Your eyes, your mirrors, and the cars that drift across a lane all behave differently after sunset.

See and be seen

Clean the windshield and the mirrors before dark, not after the glare starts. Dim interior lights so the dash is not competing with the road. If a headlight is out, the truck is harder to place in a mirror. Fix it before the next dispatch, not at the receiver.

Stay sharp

Eat something real before a night stretch, and plan the stop before you need it. If your eyes are doing the slow blink, you are already late. A dispatcher can move a check call. They cannot sleep for you.

The after-hours desk described on this site is there so someone answers while you are rolling. Use it for a problem with the load. Do not use it as a reason to skip a break.

Handling a big rig: skills new drivers need

A tractor-trailer does not steer like a pickup, and it does not stop like one. New drivers usually know that in theory. The road is where the theory gets expensive.

Space

You need more lane to turn, more road to stop, and more time to change lanes. Swing wide only as far as the turn requires, and watch the right side for the car that tries to sneak past. If a dock or a shipper looks tight, get out and look. Pride is cheaper than a fender.

The slow skills

Backing, coupling, and reading a shipper's instructions are the skills that keep a day on schedule. Rushing a blind-side back to "make the appointment" is how trailers meet poles. If you are new to a trailer type, say so. DispatchYou dispatches several trailer types, and a flatbed or a car hauler is not a dry van with different doors.

Box truck basics: what changes when you move up

A box truck feels familiar if you have driven a large van. It is still a different machine: taller, heavier, and easier to underestimate in a city lane or a low canopy.

Height and weight

Know the truck's height before you follow a car into a garage, a drive-through, or an old downtown bridge. Know what you are scaled at before you accept a load that "should fit." A dispatcher can read a posting. They cannot see the bridge from their chair unless you tell them the truck's limits.

Where it fits in dispatch

This site's equipment pages focus on tractor-trailer types: dry van, reefer, flatbed, step deck, conestoga, and car hauler, plus partial and LTL freight. A straight box truck is a different market. If that is what you run, say so when you call, so nobody quotes you a trailer percentage as if it were your truck.

Summer on the road: heat, storms, and holiday traffic

Summer adds three problems at once: heat on the driver and the tires, storms that build in the afternoon, and highways full of people who tow once a year.

Heat

Check tires when they are as cool as the day allows, and do not ignore a bulge because the truck "made it yesterday." Drink water before you feel behind. A cab that is comfortable at noon can be unsafe by four if the air stops and you decide to push the next 100 miles anyway.

Storms and crowds

If a storm is on your route, the useful call to dispatch is early: where you can wait, and whether the receiver will still take the truck. Holiday weeks need more following distance because cars stop for scenery. None of that shows up as a guaranteed smoother week. It is just how summer miles go.

Winter driving for truckers

Ice does not care that the load delivers in the morning. The useful winter skill is being willing to stop being "on time."

Before the weather arrives

Wipers, washer fluid rated for the cold, lights, and a working heater are the unglamorous list. Chains and a jacket you can actually work in belong in the truck before the forecast turns, not at a chain-up pulled from a Google search on the shoulder.

On bad pavement

Slow down earlier than you think, leave a gap you could actually stop in, and get off the road when the lane disappears. Tell the desk. A check call that says "I'm sitting this out in a truck stop" is a successful call. DispatchYou's copy talks about cutting delays. It does not tell you to outrun a storm.

Local chain laws and parking rules vary by state. Follow the rule where you are, not a general article.

What keeps a fleet's dispatch organized

Software does not dispatch a truck. It keeps the people who do from losing the thread. A fleet falls apart when the rate confirmation, the driver's location, and the invoice live in three inboxes nobody checks.

What the desk actually has to see

  • Which truck is free, and what it can haul.
  • The rate confirmation and the broker or shipper on it.
  • Check calls: arrived, loaded, rolling, delayed, empty.
  • Paper that still has to move: PODs, lumper receipts, revised appointments.

DispatchYou says it uses modern tools and sends updates on loads. The site does not name a product, and this article will not pretend a brand is required. If a tool cannot show those four things to the person on the phone, it is a notebook with a login.

What a truck dispatcher earns in a year

This article does not quote a salary. National averages move, and DispatchYou does not publish what it pays its own desk. Nothing here is an offer of employment or a promise about your truck's gross.

How the pay is usually built

Dispatchers are commonly paid in one of three ways: a salary, a percentage of the freight they book, or a fee for each load. A percentage moves with the freight. A salary does not, which is calmer and also capped. Per-load pay rewards volume and can punish a week of problem freight that takes all day and pays once.

What a carrier pays DispatchYou is a different number. That one is on the pricing page, as a percentage of the load based on fleet size, time in business, and safety score. It is not a dispatcher's wage.

Weekly dispatcher pay, without the fairy tale

No weekly dollar figure belongs in this article. If someone quotes you a guaranteed week, ask what happens when the truck sits.

Why weeks swing

A week of dispatcher pay, when it is tied to freight, follows the freight. A shutdown, a truck in the shop, or a lane that went quiet shows up immediately in percentage pay. Salary weeks look steadier and still depend on the employer having trucks to dispatch.

Carriers looking at their own week should not borrow a dispatcher's paycheck as a forecast. The only weekly figure DispatchYou prints on the homepage is an average weekly gross of $7,000+. Read it as a marketing claim about freight, not as pay for the person at the desk and not as a floor under your settlement.

Where dispatchers actually find loads

There is no secret board that only professionals can see. Dispatchers find freight where carriers find freight, and then they spend the time a driver does not have while the truck is moving.

The ordinary places

Load boards, broker calls, and shippers a carrier already knows are the usual sources. Boards show what is posted. They do not show whether the broker pays, whether the appointment is real, or whether the truck can make the pickup without breaking the driver's hours. That checking is the work.

DispatchYou's site talks about broker relationships, spot and contract rates, and checking factoring ratings before booking. It does not promise a private freight pile, and it does not name a board. If a posting sounds far above the lane, treat it as a question, not as a gift.

Per-load dispatcher earnings, explained

This describes a common pay structure. It is not DispatchYou's rate card, and it states no dollar amount per load.

Per-load pay means the dispatcher earns a set fee, or a slice, each time a load is booked and moved. It is easy to explain and easy to argue about. Who gets paid if the load cancels after the rate confirmation? Who gets paid if two people touched the negotiation? Those answers belong in the agreement, not in a blog post.

For a carrier hiring DispatchYou, the published model is a percentage of the freight, not a menu of per-load dispatcher wages. Read the pricing page for the bands. Ask the desk before you assume a cancelled load or a partial is billed the same way as a delivered truckload.

Truck dispatcher salary: the full picture

No salary survey numbers are repeated here. If you need a current market range, use a primary source and the date on it. This page will not invent one.

What changes the number

Experience, shift, and whether the pay is salary or commission change a dispatcher's year more than the job title does. Nights and weekends are a different job from a daytime desk, which is why carriers post them separately. A percentage arrangement can out-earn a salary in a strong freight month and under-earn it when trucks sit.

Do not confuse it with the carrier's fee

What you pay a dispatch company and what that company pays its people are two conversations. Your fee, if you use the model on this site, is the percentage built from fleet size, time in business, and safety score. Their payroll is theirs.

IFTA reporting, in plain language

IFTA is the agreement that lets interstate carriers file fuel tax through their base jurisdiction instead of filing a separate return in every state they touch. If you run in more than one state, you have almost certainly been told to "keep your miles and your fuel."

What you are collecting

You need miles by jurisdiction and gallons purchased, with the receipts to match. The return is generally quarterly. The math compares the fuel you bought with the fuel your miles suggest you burned in each place, and you settle the difference. The rate for each jurisdiction is not something this article should freeze in a sentence. Use the current IFTA tax rate table when you file.

Where DispatchYou fits

The solutions page says fuel-tax reporting is part of the back-office work the desk can take on, along with billing and compliance updates. That is help with the filing, not a promise that a return is correct if the miles were guessed. If your records are thin, say so before the quarter closes.

Tax rules are specific. This is orientation, not a filing instruction. Your base state and a tax preparer who handles carriers are the authorities that count.
